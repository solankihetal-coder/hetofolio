The Design

Revisiting the shipped experience.

I joined Playscape Shippers as a design contributor, creating graphics, writing website copy, and helping interns with selected UI decisions. My role later expanded into building the frontend with AI-assisted coding. Revisiting the live website gave me a chance to look at those contributions through one customer task—finding the right freight service and knowing how to ask about it.

Homepage

The homepage introduces the company through shipping imagery, a rotating hero, and an overview of its services. The imagery makes the industry immediately recognizable, and the four-item navigation gives visitors a clear sense of where they are. The hero’s next step changes with each slide, sending visitors to Services, Contact, or About depending on what they see first.

Services

The site presents six offerings, including air, ocean, road, and rail freight, customs clearance, and warehousing. The cards make those offerings easy to scan. On the homepage, each service card’s Read More link goes to the same general Services page, so visitors lose the context of the service they were exploring.

Contact

The Contact page brings together the company’s address, phone number, email, map, and message form. The form asks for a subject and message, leaving visitors to work out which shipment details are useful. The homepage also offers Book Appointment, which implies that visitors can reserve a time, but gives little guidance about how that time is confirmed.

Project Overview

A contribution that grew during delivery.

Playscape Shippers is a live website for a freight and logistics company. The team produced four public pages—Home, About, Services, and Contact.

I joined the team as a Product Designer. I created graphics, helped interns work through selected UI decisions, and wrote copy for parts of the site. The brief was loosely defined, and my responsibilities expanded during delivery. I later built the frontend with AI-assisted coding, adding implementation to the design and content work I was already doing.

02

Background & Problem Definition

From recognizing a service to asking about it.

In my post-launch review, I looked at the site from the perspective of someone with a shipment to plan. A visitor might recognize Ocean Freight as the right category and still have two questions. Can this company handle my shipment? What do I need to tell them to get a useful response?

The live site answers parts of those questions across separate pages. The homepage introduces the services, the Services page gives short descriptions, and Contact has a general message form. The visitor has to connect those steps and repeat context the site could carry forward.

03

Proposed Experience

Follow one service through to the inquiry response.

I’d start with ocean freight to show how the UI, copy, and frontend behavior fit together. These changes are a post-launch proposal.

Explore the service

Give the Ocean Freight card a short use case, such as “For larger shipments moving internationally by sea.”

Take the next step

Use Ask about ocean freight on the card so the visitor knows where the action leads.

Send an inquiry

Open the inquiry form with Ocean freight already selected. Prompt the visitor for pickup location, destination, cargo type, and preferred shipping date.

See the result

Confirm receipt once the inquiry has been accepted. If it fails, explain how to retry or reach the team by email.

04

Scope of the Project

Shipped contributions and the next opportunity.

Shipped work covered the public business website. My contribution spanned graphics, selected UI feedback, UX copy, and frontend implementation.

Post-launch proposal focuses on the service-to-inquiry path. I’d use it to test whether clearer service descriptions, specific CTAs, and a form that remembers the visitor’s selection make it easier to send a useful inquiry.

05

Interaction Requirements

Make the interface fulfil the promise of its copy.

For the proposed flow, visitors should be able to identify a relevant service, start an inquiry from that service, and understand whether their message was received.

The words Ask about ocean freight set an expectation. The frontend has to fulfil it by opening the right form, keeping the selected service, retaining the visitor’s details if submission fails, and communicating the result. Putting those decisions into a single flow gives the team something concrete to test together.

06

Evaluating the Proposal

Test the full task.

I’d give someone a realistic task. “You need to ship a large consignment overseas. Find a suitable service and show what you’d send to the company.”

I’d watch whether they choose ocean freight, find the inquiry action, provide the relevant shipment details, and understand what happens after submission. I’d also check whether they can recover from a failed submission without entering their details again.

This evaluation is a proposed next step. Its findings would guide revisions to the flow.

07

Delivery & Reflections

A shipped website and a clearer view of the journey.

The team shipped a four-page website. My role grew from contributing graphics, UI feedback, and copy to building the frontend.

Reviewing the live site later helped me see how individually reasonable decisions can interrupt a journey. A service card can communicate its purpose clearly and still leave a visitor stranded if its link leads somewhere generic.

08

What I Learned

Review the connections between decisions.

Working across graphics, copy, UI feedback, and frontend implementation helped me see how closely those contributions depend on one another.

Revisiting the shipped site made that connection concrete. The service description creates interest, the CTA sets an expectation, and the form needs to carry that context through to a clear result.

09

Conclusion

Give the team one concrete customer task.

If I were starting this project today, I’d agree on the shipment inquiry journey with the team before building individual sections. I’d work through the service card, CTA, form, and confirmation together, then test the full path as soon as it was clickable.

That would give design, copy, and frontend decisions the same concrete goal.
